// Part 3 · Imposter syndrome

Imposter
syndrome

if 70%+ feel it,
you're in good
company

Social engineering, run by your own brain

A psychological pattern where you downplay your achievements and believe you're secretly a fraud who doesn't deserve them.

It's not a medical condition and there's no test for it. It's sometimes called the imposter phenomenon for that reason.

Symptoms can include

  • Sensitivity to small mistakes or criticism
  • Burnout from overworking
  • Fear of success or failure

 

  • Impossibly high standards
  • Low self-confidence
  • Crediting luck or others for your success

Who does it affect?

70%+of people are estimated to feel like an imposter at some point
Allgenders are affected
AgeResearch is mixed: some studies find it eases with age, others find no link.
ExpertsIt's most common where expertise and intelligence are highly valued (hello, cyber)

As with most research here, studies vary and sometimes contradict each other.

Plot twist: worrying you're not good enough usually means you understand how big the field is. Actual frauds rarely lose sleep over it.

// Observed in the wild

Imposter syndrome
in cyber

heard any of
these lately?

Offence, defence, GRC, research. Nobody's immune.

  • High expectations. Pressure to perform and fear of falling short feed self-doubt.
  • Constantly evolving tech. New tools, TTPs, cloud, AI. Falling behind feels inevitable.
  • Fear of failure. When the stakes are high, any mistake feels like proof.
  • Isolation. Remote work means less casual praise, and more room to imagine people talking behind your back.

Spot the imposter thoughts

PatternWhat it sounds like
Self-doubt"I only got this job through luck. I'll be found out."
Fear of failure"I'm not going to find anything on this test."
Procrastination"If I don't start, I can't prove I'm bad at it."
Overworking"Extra hours will make up for my lack of skill."
Comparison"The last testers found a critical. They must be better."
Overreacting to feedback"Report QA comments mean they've figured me out."
Downplaying wins"Anyone would have found that bug."

The colleague test

Would you say that thought to a teammate who'd just done the same work? If not, it's not a fair assessment. It's the imposter talking.

// Coping strategies

highlight reel

Two patches for the imposter voice

Talk to others

Find a mentor
Someone more experienced who understands the challenges. They don't need to have answers. Listening is enough.

Get a cheerleader (or several)
A source of positivity to remind you what you're capable of and what you've already overcome. Family and friends count, not just colleagues.

Just talk
If a mentor feels like one more person to perform for, skip it. A quick chat to get the thoughts out of your head is often enough. Hearing it out loud shows you how inaccurate it sounds.

Stop the scroll

  • The comparison loop. The infinite feed shows you everyone's CVEs, bug bounty payouts and shiny tools, but never the hours, the luck or the years behind them.
  • Confirmation bias. We seek out and remember whatever "proves" we're an imposter.
  • Small doses. Cap your time. One well-known study found limiting social media to about 30 minutes a day reduced loneliness and low mood.

Source: Hunt et al., University of Pennsylvania, Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology (2018).

Feed hygiene

Mute accounts that leave you feeling worse. Set app timers. Follow people who share their failures and learning, not just their wins.

// Worksheet · Evidence collection

The cookie
jar

a closed ticket
counts as a
cookie

A method from David Goggins' book Can't Hurt Me

  1. 1

    Collect your cookies

    Notice personal wins, however small: a tough task finished, a fear faced, a kind thing you did.

  2. 2

    Fill your jar

    Write them down. Each cookie is evidence of your resilience and ability.

  3. 3

    Reach into the jar

    When doubt hits, read them back. Past wins are proof you can handle the next challenge.
