Your body's own incident response

A physical and emotional response to pressures or demands, which happens when the challenges you face exceed your perceived ability to cope.
  • Not a medical condition in itself, but persistent stress can feed into physical and mental health problems.
  • No definitive test. It's recognised through common signs and patterns (below).

The Yerkes–Dodson law

Performance improves as pressure rises, but only up to a point. Past that, more stress makes us worse, not better.

Too little
Boredom, low motivation, underperformance
Just right
Focus, energy, your best work
Too much
Overwhelm, anxiety, mistakes

"Arousal" here means how switched-on your brain and body are, nothing more. It's a simplified, widely used model, not a precise law.

Indicators of compromise for humans

Under pressure, your body releases stress hormones like adrenaline and cortisol to get you ready to fight or run. Brilliant for a two-hour incident. Not so good for a four-month project. Tick what you've noticed lately.

In your body

  • Muscle aches and headaches
  • Sleep problems
  • Fatigue
  • Feeling sick, dizzy or faint
  • Existing health problems getting worse
  • Changes to your menstrual cycle

In how you behave

  • Eating too much or too little
  • Irritability or mood swings
  • Crying or feeling tearful
  • Unable to concentrate
  • Grinding teeth, clenching your jaw
  • Withdrawing from people

Bored-out is real too

Look at the left side of the curve. Weeks with nothing to do, such as waiting on clearances, sitting "on the bench" between projects, or a role that's gone quiet, can be just as draining as overwork. Low motivation, skipping exercise, drinking more and worrying that you're not needed are all common there.

What is
anxiety?

a false positive
still feels real

When the alarm keeps ringing after the threat has gone

A psychological and physical response involving persistent worry, fear or unease, often out of proportion to the situation or without a clear trigger.

Anxiety isn't always a medical condition. But ongoing or intense anxiety can be part of an anxiety disorder, such as generalised anxiety disorder (GAD), which deserves a professional assessment.

In your body

  • A churning stomach
  • Sleep problems
  • Faster breathing
  • Fast, thumping or irregular heartbeat
  • Panic attacks
  • Restless, can't sit still

In your mind

  • Tense, nervous, can't relax
  • Low mood
  • Constant need for reassurance
  • Worrying people are upset with you
  • Rumination
  • Worrying about what might happen
Panic attack: a sudden wave of intense fear with strong physical reactions when there's no real danger. It can feel like losing control or a heart attack. If you're ever unsure whether it's panic or your heart, get medical help.

Grounding: the 5-4-3-2-1 scan

When anxiety spirals, slowly name what's around you: 5 things you can see (a mug, a window, your keyboard), 4 things you can touch (your feet on the floor, the chair at your back), 3 things you can hear (a laptop fan, traffic, your breathing), 2 things you can smell (coffee, soap) and 1 thing you can taste (a sip of water). It pulls your attention back into the present.

Rumination: replaying a bad experience or situation over and over, like a failed exploit stuck in a loop.

Stress and anxiety threat actors in our industry

Chronic workload stress CRITICAL

Continuous monitoring and alert queues that never end. "Always on alert" slowly becomes vigilance fatigue, exhaustion and burnout.

Back-to-back engagements CRITICAL

A new client, scope and tech stack every week or two, with utilisation targets and report deadlines stacking up behind each test. There's rarely time to recover in between.

Acute incident stress HIGH

Responding to breaches creates high-pressure moments: adrenaline spikes, broken sleep and mental fatigue, often outside working hours.

The "empty report" fear HIGH

Days into a test with nothing to show. Clients expect findings, and a clean report can feel like proof you missed something, even when the environment is genuinely solid.

Fear of failure anxiety HIGH

A single mistake could mean a breach, financial loss or reputational damage. And blame often lands on people, not systemic failures.

Organisational pressure MEDIUM

Conflicting priorities: strong security without disrupting the business, on a tight budget, explained to non-technical stakeholders.

None of this means you're in the wrong job. It means the job has real hazards, and hazards need controls.

Scheduling is a security control

  1. 1

    Find your best time of day

    Do high-focus work when you're most alert: mornings if you're a morning person, evenings if you're not.

  2. 2

    List and rank your tasks

    Most important first. Timetable when you'll tackle each one. Ask a manager or colleague to help prioritise, and postpone what isn't urgent.

  3. 3

    Don't take on too much at once

    Fewer tasks, each done well, beats ten tasks done badly in parallel.

  4. 4

    Take regular breaks, keep a steady pace

    Hard to do when stressed. That's exactly when it helps most.

  5. 5

    Ask someone to help!

    Delegating frees time for the tasks that are actually causing the stress. Asking isn't weakness, it's load balancing.

Why it works

  • gives a sense of control
  • prevents last-minute pressure
  • keeps focus on what matters
  • protects work–life balance
  • reduces mental overload

Awareness is the first step to control

Knowing what sets you off lets you anticipate problems and prepare, even when you can't avoid the situation.

  • Track your patterns. Keep a short log of stressful moments to spot recurring triggers.
  • Controllable vs. uncontrollable. Spend energy where you have influence, not on what's outside your control.
  • Check the environment. Noise, interruptions and bad ergonomics count. Triggers aren't always tasks.
  • Recognise emotional triggers. Fear of failure, perfectionism, feeling unprepared.
  • Use triggers as signals. Once you know them, they become early alerts to start coping before it's overwhelming.

Boring controls that block most attacks

01 · Eat right

Proper nutrition fuels your body and stabilises your mood. Regular meals, away from the keyboard.

02 · Exercise

Physical activity reduces stress and clears your head. Even a 10-minute walk counts.

03 · Sleep right

Quality sleep restores your mind and builds resilience. Most adults need 7–9 hours. Screens off before bed helps.

04 · Speak to someone

Sharing how you feel eases the load. Friends, family, a colleague you trust.

05 · Seek professional advice

A GP or therapist can give you proper tools. You don't need to wait until it's a crisis.

Alcohol & nicotine: a false patch

They feel like relief in the moment, but they create a cycle of dependency. Nicotine withdrawal raises anxiety, and alcohol disrupts sleep and lowers mood. Over time both make stress, anxiety and depression worse.
